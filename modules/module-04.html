The Big Picture

  • Disruptive technologies start out worse at what current customers care about, then improve until they take over the market.
  • Big, successful firms often miss disruption because they listen to their best customers and chase their most profitable products.
  • To survive, firms should watch for disruptors, experiment in small, separate teams, and avoid judging new ideas only by old numbers.
  • 3D printing (additive manufacturing) didn't take over homes, but it's disrupting areas like construction, medical implants, and aerospace parts.
  • Crypto and blockchain show real promise and also massive hype, scams, and collapses like FTX.

Section 4.1

Understanding Truly Disruptive Innovation

Harvard professor Clayton Christensen studied why industry giants get toppled. He found that disruptive technologies share two traits:

Trait 1

They start out “worse”

They come to market with features that current mainstream customers don't value, so leaders ignore them.

Trait 2

They get better fast

Their performance improves until they're good enough to invade the established market and win.

Why big firms fail

Leaders aren't lazy. They make sensible choices: they listen to their best customers, protect high-profit products, and skip small, low-margin markets. Those sensible choices are exactly why they miss the disruptor.

Real-world example · Kodak

Kodak engineers built one of the first digital cameras, but the company's profits came from selling film. Early digital photos looked worse, and Kodak's customers wanted film. Digital kept improving, then smartphones arrived, and Kodak ended up in bankruptcy.

Disruption in progress · Intel, ARM & Nvidia

Intel dominated PC chips. But ARM's low-power chip designs took over phones, and now Apple and others use them in laptops too. Meanwhile, Nvidia's graphics chips, first built for video games, turned out to be ideal for AI, and Nvidia became one of the world's most valuable companies. That's disruption from two directions.

Section 4.2

Recognizing and Responding to Disruption

  1. Improve your radarTrack startups, research labs, and fringe customers. Ask what's “not good enough yet” but improving fast.
  2. Don't judge only by old numbersBeware the McNamara fallacy: deciding based only on past, measurable data when the market itself is changing.
  3. Experiment in a separate unitGive new ideas a small team with its own goals and KPIs, so they aren't crushed by the main business's profit targets.
  4. Be willing to cannibalize yourselfIt's better to replace your own product than to let a rival do it.
Missed it

Yahoo!

Yahoo! was an early web giant but was slow to commit to mobile. Rivals that put mobile first captured the next generation of users.

Got it

Intuit

The maker of TurboTax and QuickBooks runs lots of small, fast experiments and backs the winners, which helped it move to cloud and mobile.

Real-world example · Robot kitchens

Salad chain sweetgreen bought Spyce, a robotics startup, and now runs automated “Infinite Kitchens” that assemble bowls with machines. Could robots disrupt restaurant labor? It's worth watching.

Section 4.3

Tech to Consider: Additive Manufacturing (3D Printing)

Traditional manufacturing carves or molds parts from a block of material. Additive manufacturing builds parts layer by layer, adding material only where it's needed. Predictions of a 3D printer in every home didn't come true, but industrial uses are growing.

When does additive manufacturing make sense?
Good fitWeaker fit
Custom, one-off parts (like a hip implant shaped for one patient)Making millions of identical items cheaply
Complex shapes that are impossible to machineSimple parts that molding already makes fast
Prototypes and design testingMaterials that printers can't handle well
Making parts on-site (no shipping or inventory)Jobs where speed per unit matters most
Real-world example · 3D-printed houses

Companies like SQ4D print the concrete walls of a home on-site in days, with less waste and labor. If it scales, it could disrupt home building and help with housing shortages. Firms like VulcanForms also print precision metal parts for aerospace and medical customers.

Section 4.4

Tech to Consider: Crypto, Blockchain & DeFi

A blockchain is a shared digital ledger copied across many computers. Each new “block” of records is linked to the one before, so changing old records is extremely hard. That's why people call a blockchain immutable.

Crypto

Bitcoin

A decentralized digital payment system with no bank or government in charge, running on a blockchain.

Crypto

Stablecoins

Cryptocurrencies whose value is pegged to a currency like the U.S. dollar, so they don't swing wildly in price.

Government

CBDCs

Central bank digital currencies, like China's e-CNY: digital versions of official government money.

Finance

DeFi

Decentralized finance: lending, trading, and other services run on blockchains without a bank in the middle.

Ownership

NFTs

Non-fungible tokens record ownership of a digital item on a blockchain. They create scarcity, like owning the one original painting.

Promising uses · Tracking food

Some companies use blockchains to track food from farm to store. If there's a contamination outbreak, they can trace the source in seconds instead of days.

Watch out · The FTX collapse

FTX was one of the biggest crypto exchanges until it collapsed in 2022, the largest crypto bankruptcy at the time. Its founder was later convicted of fraud for misusing customer money. The scandal helped trigger a “crypto winter” and shows how little protection crypto users can have compared with regulated banks.

Think like a manager

Ask the disruption questions: Who is this for? What problem does it solve better than today's options? Is it improving fast? Hype isn't the same thing as value.

Vocabulary

Key Terms

Want every term from every module in one place? Try the searchable glossary.

Disruptive technology
An innovation that starts out with features mainstream customers don't value, then improves until it takes over the market.
McNamara fallacy
Making decisions based only on past, measurable data and ignoring changes the numbers don't capture yet.
KPI
Key performance indicator: a measurable value a firm uses to track progress toward a goal.
Cannibalize
When a firm's new product takes sales from its own existing product.
Additive manufacturing
Industrial 3D printing that builds an object layer by layer, adding material only where needed.
Bitcoin
A decentralized, peer-to-peer digital payment system that runs without a bank or central authority.
Cryptocurrency
A digital asset that uses cryptography (secure math) to verify transactions and control the creation of new units.
Blockchain
A decentralized digital ledger, copied across many computers, that records transactions and is very hard to tamper with.
DeFi
Decentralized finance: financial services built on blockchains that work without a bank or government in the middle.
Stablecoin
A cryptocurrency whose value is pegged to a currency or commodity, like the U.S. dollar.
CBDC
Central bank digital currency: a digital form of a government-issued currency.
NFT
Non-fungible token: a blockchain record that proves ownership of a unique digital item.
