The Big Picture

  • Security breaches are rising and can be devastating. Security has to be a top priority for the whole organization, not just IT.
  • Attackers range from organized criminals and nation-states to hacktivists and unhappy insiders, with motives like money, espionage, politics, and revenge.
  • The weakest link is often people. Social engineering and phishing trick users into handing over access, and AI deepfakes make scams more convincing.
  • Technical threats include malware, zero-day exploits, SQL injection, botnets, and insecure IoT devices.
  • Defense takes layers: strong passwords or passkeys, multi-factor authentication, encryption, patching, firewalls, training, audits, and constant vigilance.

Section 15.1

You’ve Probably Already Been Hacked

If you have a bank account, a credit card, or an online account, your data has very likely been exposed in at least one breach. Attacks cost firms money, customers, reputation, and sometimes their executives' jobs.

Case · The Target hack

Attackers got into Target's network using login credentials stolen from a heating-and-air-conditioning vendor, then installed malware on checkout terminals. They stole data from about 40 million payment cards during the 2013 holiday season. Target's security tools raised alerts, but staff didn't act on them in time. The CEO later resigned.

Case · Log4j

In late 2021, a flaw was found in Log4j, a free, open source logging tool hidden inside millions of systems. It let attackers take over computers remotely. Organizations scrambled to find every place they used it, a reminder that software you don't even know you have can put you at risk.

Section 15.2

Who Is Doing It, and Why?

Money

Cybercriminals

Data harvesters steal data and sell it. Cash-out fraudsters buy it and use it to make purchases, open fake accounts, or empty bank accounts.

Power

Nation-states

Governments use cyberattacks for spying, sabotage, and election interference. That's called cyberwarfare.

Politics

Hacktivists

Protesters who break into or deface systems to make a political point.

Inside

Bad apples

Employees or contractors who misuse their access, whether on purpose or by accident.

Real-world example · Stuxnet

Stuxnet, discovered in 2010, was malware widely believed to have been built by the U.S. and Israel. It secretly damaged centrifuges at an Iranian nuclear facility. It showed that code could cause physical destruction and started a new era of cyberwarfare.

Section 15.3

Vulnerabilities, Part 1: People

Social engineering means tricking people instead of hacking machines. Common tricks include pretending to be the IT help desk, creating fake urgency, dumpster diving through trash, and shoulder surfing to see someone type a password.

Spot the phish

Phishing uses fake emails, texts, or sites to steal information or install malware. Spear phishing targets a specific person or organization using personal details to look believable. Messages are often spoofed to look like they come from someone you trust.

  1. Urgency or fear“Your account will be closed in 24 hours!”
  2. Mismatched linksHover over a link. Does the real address match the company?
  3. Requests for credentialsReal companies rarely ask for your password by email or text.
  4. Too good to be truePrizes, refunds, or “gift card” requests from the boss.
Watch out · AI supercharges scams

AI writes flawless phishing emails in any language, and deepfakes can clone a voice or face. Scammers have used fake video calls of executives to trick employees into wiring millions. If a request involves money or access, verify it through a separate channel.

Passwords, MFA, and passkeys

Do

Long and unique

Use a long passphrase and a different password for every site, kept in a password manager. Short passwords fall quickly to brute-force attacks.

Do

Turn on MFA

Multi-factor authentication (like 2FA) requires something you know plus something you have or are, like a phone code or a fingerprint.

Better

Passkeys

Passkeys replace passwords with cryptographic keys stored on your device and unlocked with your face, fingerprint, or PIN. They're very hard to phish.

Also

Biometrics

Fingerprints, face scans, and voice-prints identify you by your body. They're convenient, but you can't change your face if the data leaks.

Vulnerabilities, Part 2: Technology

TrojanPretends to be something useful while hiding a malicious purpose

For web developers · How SQL injection works

A poorly coded login form might paste whatever you type directly into a database query. An attacker types this as their password:

' OR '1'='1

The query becomes “...WHERE password = '' OR '1'='1'”. Since '1'='1' is always true, the attacker gets in. The fix: never trust user input. Use parameterized queries (prepared statements) and validate everything.

Supply chain

SolarWinds

In 2020, hackers slipped malicious code into a trusted software update from the IT company SolarWinds and used it to get into thousands of organizations, including U.S. government agencies.

IoT

Swiss cheese security

Cameras, smart speakers, and appliances often ship with weak default passwords and are rarely updated, which makes them easy targets for botnets.

AI

Push-button hacking

Criminal AI tools like “WormGPT” help unskilled attackers write malware and scams.

Physical

Don't forget the hardware

Stolen laptops, unlocked server rooms, and plug-in devices are threats too.

Key idea · Encryption

Encryption scrambles data so only someone with the key can read it. Public-key cryptography uses a key pair: a public key locks the data, and only the matching private key unlocks it. It's what secures HTTPS, and it's why a stolen encrypted phone is mostly useless to a thief.

Section 15.4

Taking Action

As a user

  • Use a password manager, unique passphrases, and MFA or passkeys everywhere you can.
  • Install updates promptly. Most attacks use flaws that already have fixes.
  • Think before you click. Verify unusual requests through another channel.
  • Back up important files, encrypt your devices, and be careful on public Wi-Fi.
  • Limit what you share on social media. Attackers use it to craft believable scams.

As an organization

Tech

Layered defenses

Firewalls, intrusion detection systems, honeypots (decoy targets), blacklists (block known bad senders), and whitelists (allow only approved ones).

People

Education

Regular training and practice phishing tests. Everyone, including executives, is a target.

Process

Audit & enforce

Follow security frameworks and standards. Have red teams probe your defenses, and use AI to spot unusual activity.

Watch out · Compliance ≠ security

Payment processor Heartland had passed an industry security audit, yet it suffered one of the largest card breaches ever. Checking the boxes on a standard isn't the same as being secure. Security is an ongoing process, not a certificate.

Vocabulary

Key Terms

Want every term from every module in one place? Try the searchable glossary.

Data harvesters
Cybercriminals who break into systems to steal data and resell it.
Cash-out fraudsters
Criminals who buy stolen data and use it for financial gain.
Botnet
A network of infected computers secretly controlled by an attacker. Also called a zombie network.
DDoS attack
Distributed denial of service: flooding a system with traffic until it slows down or crashes.
Hacktivist
Someone who uses hacking to make a political point.
White hat hacker
An ethical hacker who finds security weaknesses so they can be fixed.
Black hat hacker
A computer criminal.
Red team
An approved group that plays the attacker to find weaknesses.
Blue team
Defensive security staff who protect an organization's systems.
Social engineering
Manipulating people into revealing information or granting access.
Phishing
A con using fake emails, texts, or sites to steal information or install malware.
Spear phishing
Phishing that targets a specific person or organization.
Spoofing
Faking the origin of an email or data so it looks like it came from someone else.
Zero-day exploit
An attack on a flaw so new that no fix or detection exists yet.
Malware
Malicious software such as viruses, worms, trojans, ransomware, and spyware.
Ransomware
Malware that encrypts a victim's files and demands payment to unlock them.
SQL injection
An attack that sneaks database commands through a website's input fields.
Script kiddie
An unskilled attacker who uses ready-made hacking tools.
Brute-force attack
Trying every possible password combination until one works.
Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
Proving your identity with more than one factor, like a password plus a phone code.
Passkey
A password replacement that uses cryptographic keys on your device, unlocked with a biometric or PIN.
Biometrics
Identifying people by body characteristics like fingerprints, faces, or voices.
Encryption
Scrambling data so only someone with the right key can read it.
Public-key cryptography
Encryption that uses a public key to lock data and a matching private key to unlock it.
Firewall
A system that blocks unauthorized network traffic while allowing approved traffic.
Intrusion detection system
A system that monitors network activity for signs of attack.
Honeypot
A fake, tempting target used to lure attackers and study their methods.
Dumpster diving
Searching through trash for valuable information.
Shoulder surfing
Watching someone, for example over their shoulder, to steal information like a password.
