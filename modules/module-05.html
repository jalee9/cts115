The Big Picture

  • Amazon competes in retail, logistics, devices, media, advertising, AI, and cloud computing. It's an empire built on long-term thinking and constant experimentation.
  • Robots, AI, and data make Amazon's warehouses fast and cheap. Selling goods before it pays suppliers gives it a negative cash conversion cycle.
  • Huge selection, a two-sided Marketplace, and Prime create network effects and switching costs.
  • AWS, Amazon's cloud business, is the leading cloud provider and earns a large share of Amazon's profits.
  • Amazon's size draws government scrutiny. Shopify shows a different model: helping merchants sell on their own.
Your goals

What You’ll Learn

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  • Describe how warehouse robotics and AI help Amazon get products to customers quickly and cheaply.
  • Explain inventory turns, accounts payable, and why a negative cash conversion cycle is good.
  • Explain how data (A/B tests, personalization, advertising) and the long tail strengthen Amazon.
  • Identify the two sides of the Amazon Marketplace network effect.
  • Explain why Amazon builds its own logistics, devices, and media businesses, and what channel conflict is.
  • Describe AWS, key cloud terms, and how Amazon sells AI to other firms.
  • Compare Amazon's model with Shopify's, including drop shipping.
Section 5.1

Why Study Amazon?

Amazon started in 1994 as an online bookstore in Jeff Bezos's garage. Today it's one of the world's most valuable companies. Andy Jassy, who built Amazon Web Services (AWS), replaced Bezos as CEO in 2021. Amazon's profits have swung up and down over the years because the firm repeatedly sacrifices short-term profit for long-term growth.

Mindset

Think long-term

Bezos told investors to expect years of heavy investment before payoffs. Most rivals answer to quarterly results.

Mindset

Customer obsession

Low prices, huge selection, and fast delivery come first. Amazon assumes profit follows loyal customers.

Mindset

Experiment constantly

Small “two-pizza teams” (small enough to feed with two pizzas) test ideas quickly, so the giant can still act like a startup.

Section 5.2

The Emperor of E-commerce: Robots, Cash, and Data

Robots and AI in the warehouse

Amazon's fulfillment centers use robots that carry entire shelves to human pickers (originally made by Kiva, a company Amazon bought), fully autonomous robots like Proteus that move safely among workers, and robotic arms like Sparrow that grab individual items. Computer vision checks that items go in the right bins. Amazon also experiments with gamification, turning work tasks into games to boost engagement.

The cash conversion cycle: getting paid first

Many retailers pay suppliers before products sell, so they need loans and can run into liquidity problems. Amazon's negative cash conversion cycle works like an interest-free loan from its suppliers, and AI demand forecasting keeps inventory moving even faster.

Data is a superpower

Data

A/B testing

Show two versions of a page to random groups and keep the one that performs better. It's easy to do online and nearly impossible in a physical store.

Data

Personalization

Collaborative filtering powers “customers who bought this also bought…” recommendations.

Data

Ad business

Amazon knows what people actually buy, so brands pay for ads there. Its ad business ranks behind only Google and Meta.

Data

Dynamic pricing

Prices shift automatically as demand, competition, and supply change.

Selection and network effects

The Amazon Marketplace lets outside sellers list products next to Amazon's own. More buyers attract more sellers, and more sellers attract more buyers. That's a two-sided network effect. The result is a nearly endless selection, called the long tail: lots of niche products that each sell a little but add up to a lot.

Section 5.3

The Lord of Logistics

Logistics is how goods are acquired, stored, and moved. Amazon used to rely on UPS, FedEx, and USPS. Now it owns much of the chain itself, including cargo planes, ships, trucks, and local delivery partners. Owning more steps of the chain like this is called vertical integration.

  1. First mileData predicts what will sell where. Amazon orders in bulk and ships it across oceans in its own containers.
  2. Middle mileIts own planes and trucks move goods between fulfillment and sorting centers.
  3. Last mileSmaller same-day sites, delivery vans, lockers, and AI-planned routes get packages to doorsteps faster.
Why it matters

Controlling logistics lowers fulfillment costs, speeds delivery, and protects Amazon during supply-chain crises. It could even make Amazon a delivery rival to UPS and FedEx.

Sections 5.4–5.5

Physical Stores, Devices, and Media

Not every experiment works. Amazon has closed several store concepts, including bookstores and 4-star shops. But it keeps testing AI-powered retail:

Grocery

Whole Foods

Amazon bought Whole Foods in 2017, lowered prices, and added Prime perks and pay-with-your-palm checkout.

AI

Just Walk Out

Cameras and weight sensors track what you take, so you leave without a checkout line. Amazon sells this tech to other retailers.

Devices

Kindle, Fire, Alexa

Devices are often sold near cost. They pull customers into Amazon's ecosystem of books, video, and shopping. Alexa is popular but has lost a lot of money.

Media

Twitch, Prime Video & publishing

Amazon is now a major content publisher and is adding ads to Prime Video.

Watch out · The Fire Phone flop

Amazon's Fire Phone arrived years after the iPhone and Android, without enough apps or a reason to switch. It shows that late movers need a real advantage, not just a famous brand.

Key concept · Channel conflict

Channel conflict happens when a firm's partners see it as a threat. Publishers sell books through Amazon, but Amazon also publishes its own books and pushes for lower e-book prices. Partners depend on Amazon while competing with it.

Section 5.6

Amazon and the Cloud: AWS

Amazon Web Services rents computing power, storage, databases, and AI tools over the Internet. It's the market leader in cloud computing and one of Amazon's most profitable businesses. Netflix, NASA, and countless startups run on it.

AI at Amazon, and AI for sale

Amazon uses AI everywhere, from forecasting and delivery routes to recommendations. Through AWS, it sells AI to other firms: Bedrock (build with many AI foundation models while keeping data private), SageMaker (train your own models), and Amazon Q (a secure chatbot for businesses). It designs its own AI chips and has invested billions in the AI firm Anthropic.

Sections 5.8–5.9

Is Amazon Too Big? And a Different Model: Shopify

In 2023, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) sued Amazon. It argued that Amazon uses its power to hurt both shoppers and the outside sellers who depend on its marketplace. Regulators worldwide are watching how Amazon treats sellers that are also its competitors.

Amazon vs. Shopify

Key concept · Drop shipping

Many Shopify stores use drop shipping: the store takes the order, and a supplier ships the product directly to the customer. The seller never touches inventory. It's easy to start, but margins are thin, and most new stores quit quickly. Shopify grew fast during the pandemic, then had to cut back.

Vocabulary

Key Terms

Want every term from every module in one place? Try the searchable glossary.

Inventory turns
How many times a firm sells through its inventory in a period. Higher means products sell quickly.
Accounts payable
Money a firm owes for goods or services it bought on credit.
Cash conversion cycle
The time between paying out cash for goods and collecting cash from selling them. Negative means you're paid before you pay.
Liquidity problems
Trouble turning assets into cash quickly enough to pay bills.
Deep learning
A type of machine learning that uses many layers of connections (neural networks) to find patterns and make predictions.
Dynamic pricing
Prices that change over time based on demand, supply, or competition.
A/B test
An experiment that randomly shows two versions to different groups and compares which performs better.
Collaborative filtering
Software that uses patterns across many customers to personalize recommendations.
Two-sided network effect
When a service gets more valuable as two different groups grow, such as buyers and sellers.
Long tail
Making money by offering a huge selection of niche products that each sell in small amounts.
Gamification
Adding game elements like points, levels, or challenges to tasks to boost engagement.
Logistics
Managing how goods are acquired, stored, and moved to their final destination.
Fulfillment costs
The costs to receive, pack, and ship orders.
Channel conflict
When a firm's partners see it as a threat because it competes with them.
Thin device
A device with little computing power that relies on the cloud for most of its work.
Virtual machine
A software-based computer that runs like a physical one, often in the cloud.
Bursting
Using extra cloud capacity during periods of high demand.
Hybrid cloud
A setup that combines a firm's own servers with public cloud services.
Serverless
A cloud model where developers just write code and the provider manages all the servers.
Foundation model
A general-purpose AI model that can be adapted for many specific tasks.
Drop shipping
Selling products that a supplier ships directly to the customer, so the seller never holds inventory.
