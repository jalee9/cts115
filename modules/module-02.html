The Big Picture

  • Doing the same things better than rivals (operational effectiveness) helps, but it's easy to copy, so it rarely lasts.
  • A lasting edge usually comes from strategic positioning: doing different things, or doing things differently.
  • Resources that create sustainable competitive advantage must be valuable, rare, hard to imitate, and hard to substitute.
  • Powerful resources include brand, scale, switching costs, data, differentiation, network effects, and distribution channels. Technology can build every one of them.
  • Porter's Five Forces shows how attractive an industry is and how technology shifts power between firms, buyers, and suppliers.

Section 2.1

Why Technology Alone Isn’t a Strategy

Firms want sustainable competitive advantage, which means beating the industry's average financial performance year after year. But simply buying the latest technology rarely gets you there. If you can buy it, so can your rivals.

Two ways to compete
Operational effectivenessStrategic positioning
What it meansDoing the same tasks better than rivalsDoing different tasks, or the same tasks in a different way
ExampleA faster checkout system or cheaper softwareA unique business model that rivals can't easily copy
How long it lastsUsually short. Rivals copy best practices and tech quickly.Can last for years if it's built on hard-to-copy resources
Watch out · The commodity trap

When every rival offers nearly the same thing, the product becomes a commodity, like milk or coal. Customers then choose on price alone, and profits shrink. Tech that rivals can buy off the shelf pushes firms toward this trap.

Real-world example · TiVo

TiVo pioneered the digital video recorder and had a beloved product. But cable companies, other hardware makers, and later streaming services offered similar features, often bundled into services customers already paid for. Being first and having great tech wasn't enough to build a lasting business. That's the fast follower problem in action.

The resource-based view: four tests for a lasting advantage

According to the resource-based view, a firm keeps its edge only if it controls resources that pass all four of these tests:

1

Valuable

It helps the firm earn more or spend less.

2

Rare

Few or no rivals have it.

3

Imperfectly imitable

It's difficult or expensive for others to copy.

4

Non-substitutable

Rivals can't swap in something different that works just as well.

Section 2.2

The Value Chain: How a Firm Delivers

The value chain is the set of activities a firm uses to create a product or service and get it to customers. Comparing value chains shows how firms differ, and where technology can build an imitation-resistant value chain that rivals struggle to copy.

Real-world example · Dell

Dell grew by selling PCs directly to customers and building each one to order. That value chain kept inventory low and cash flowing. But when shoppers shifted to laptops bought in stores, and rivals matched Dell's efficiency, the advantage faded. Nothing lasts forever, so firms must keep re-examining their value chain.

Watch out · Straddling

A firm that tries to occupy two positions at once (for example, low-cost and full-service) often ends up worse than a rival that focuses on just one. This is called straddling.

Section 2.2

Seven Powerful Resources (and How Tech Builds Them)

Brand

What people think of you

Everything customers associate with a product. Tech firms often build brands through viral marketing, where users spread the word.

Scale

Bigger can be cheaper

Economies of scale spread costs over more customers. Software and Internet firms can serve millions more users at little extra cost.

Switching costs

Hard to leave

The time, money, or effort it takes to switch products, like learning new software or moving your data and playlists.

Data

Knowing customers

Data gathered over time lets a firm personalize, predict, and improve. A newcomer can't easily copy that history.

Differentiation

Being different

Offering something customers value that rivals don't, so the firm can compete on more than price.

Network effects

More users, more value

The product gets more valuable as more people use it. (Much more in Module 7.)

Distribution

Reaching customers

Distribution channels are the paths products take to customers. Owning or controlling a channel is a big edge.

Real-world example · OpenTable

OpenTable gave restaurants reservation software, then let diners book tables online. More diners attracted more restaurants, and more restaurants attracted more diners. Add the switching cost of a restaurant retraining staff on a new system, and rivals found it very hard to catch up.

Real-world example · Google pays to be the default

Google pays Apple billions each year to be the default search engine on iPhones and Safari. That's a distribution channel deal, and it's so powerful that U.S. regulators challenged it in court as anticompetitive.

Other tools firms use: bundling (selling products together as one package), APIs (published rules that let other programs connect to a service), and patents. Watch out for non-practicing entities (“patent trolls”), which make money by suing over patents instead of building products.

Section 2.3

Timing, Technology, and Barriers to Entry

Is it better to be first? Not necessarily. First movers can build brand, scale, and switching costs early. But fast followers can learn from the pioneer's mistakes and enter with a better, cheaper product.

Myth

“First mover always wins.”

Google wasn't the first search engine. Facebook wasn't the first social network. Both arrived later and won.

Reality

Resources matter more than timing

Timing only helps if the firm uses its head start to build resources that are hard to copy.

Key idea

Low barriers ≠ easy wins

If it's cheap and easy for you to start a business, it's cheap and easy for copycats too.

Section 2.4

Key Framework: Porter’s Five Forces

Michael Porter's Five Forces framework sizes up how attractive (profitable) an industry is. The stronger the forces, the harder it is to earn high profits. Technology can strengthen or weaken each force.

How technology can shift each force
ForceThe questionTech example
RivalryHow fiercely do current competitors fight, often on price?Price-comparison sites make rivals compete harder on price.
New entrantsHow easily can newcomers enter?Cloud computing lowers startup costs, so more entrants appear.
SubstitutesCan customers meet the need a different way?Streaming replaced DVDs. GenAI chatbots replace some web searches and help forums.
Supplier powerCan suppliers raise prices on you?Only a few firms make advanced AI chips, so chip suppliers hold a lot of power.
Buyer powerCan customers push your prices down?Online reviews and price transparency give shoppers more power.
Real-world example · ChatGPT vs. Stack Overflow

Stack Overflow was the go-to Q&A site for programmers. When AI chatbots began answering coding questions instantly, traffic to the site dropped sharply. A new substitute appeared almost overnight.

Why it matters to you

The Internet reduces information asymmetry, where one side knows more than the other. When you research a car's dealer cost online before you shop, buyer power shifts toward you.

Vocabulary

Key Terms

Want every term from every module in one place? Try the searchable glossary.

Sustainable competitive advantage
Financial performance that consistently beats the industry average over time.
Operational effectiveness
Doing the same tasks better than rivals do. Useful, but usually easy to copy.
Strategic positioning
Doing different tasks than rivals, or the same tasks in a different way.
Commodity
A basic product that's nearly interchangeable with competitors' versions, so buyers choose mostly on price.
Fast follower problem
When rivals watch a pioneer, learn from its mistakes, and quickly launch a similar or better product for less.
Resource-based view
The idea that lasting advantage comes from controlling resources that are valuable, rare, hard to imitate, and hard to substitute.
Value chain
The set of activities a firm uses to create a product or service and deliver it to customers.
Imitation-resistant value chain
A way of doing business, often enabled by technology, that competitors find very hard to copy.
Straddling
Trying to hold two strategic positions at once and doing worse than a rival focused on just one.
Brand
Everything customers know, feel, and associate with a product or company.
Viral marketing
Getting customers to promote a product to others, often by word of mouth or social sharing.
Economies of scale
When costs are spread over more units or customers, so each one costs less to serve.
Switching costs
The money, time, or effort a customer must spend to move from one product to another.
Network effects
When a product becomes more valuable as more people use it. Also called Metcalfe's Law.
Distribution channels
The paths through which products or services reach customers.
Bundling
Selling separate products together as one package.
API
A published set of rules that lets other programs connect to and use a service.
Non-practicing entity
A “patent troll”: a firm that makes money by acquiring patents and suing others instead of making products.
Porter's Five Forces
A framework that judges an industry's attractiveness using rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, supplier power, and buyer power.
Price transparency
How much complete price information is available to buyers.
Information asymmetry
When one side of a deal knows more, or has better information, than the other.
