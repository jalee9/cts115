The Big Picture

  • Network effects: a product gets more valuable as more people use it. Think messaging apps, game consoles, and marketplaces.
  • Network value comes from three sources: exchange, staying power, and complementary benefits.
  • Markets can be one-sided (users benefit from other users) or two-sided (two groups, like buyers and sellers, need each other).
  • Strong network effects often create winner-take-all markets where a late, incompatible rival struggles to compete.
  • Firms compete by moving early, subsidizing adoption, going viral, building alliances, and encouraging complementary products, or by leapfrogging with something far better.

Section 7.1

What Are Network Effects?

A phone is useless if you're the only person who has one. With every new user, each phone becomes more valuable. That's a network effect, also called Metcalfe's Law. A product with network effects can become a platform: a base that others build products and services on, like Windows, iOS, or a game console.

Three sources of network value

1

Exchange

More people to message, trade with, play with, or share files with.

2

Staying power

Users trust a big network to last, so they invest in it. High switching costs keep them there.

3

Complementary benefits

Popular platforms attract apps, accessories, and services built for them, which makes the platform even more useful.

Real-world example · Roblox

Roblox gives users tools to build their own games, many of them created by teens. More creators make more games, more games attract more players, and more players attract more creators. The players are also the platform builders.

Section 7.3

One-Sided or Two-Sided Markets?

Comparing network structures
One-sided marketTwo-sided market
Who's in it?One kind of userTwo distinct groups who need each other
Benefit typeSame-side: users gain from more users like themCross-side: growth on one side attracts the other side
ExamplesMessaging apps, phone networksGame consoles (players and game makers), Uber (riders and drivers), app stores
Real-world example · EV charging: everyone vs. Tesla

Electric cars and charging stations form a two-sided market. Tesla built the largest, most reliable fast-charging network with its own plug. Most major automakers have since agreed to adopt Tesla's plug standard (now called NACS) so their drivers can use it. It's a striking example of a network becoming the standard.

Section 7.4

How These Markets Are Different: Winner-Take-All

In markets with strong network effects, customers flock to the biggest network, so markets “tip” toward a single winner (a monopoly) or a few (an oligopoly). A late rival with an incompatible product must overcome the leader's exchange value, switching costs, and library of complementary products.

The one big exception · Leapfrogging

Technological leapfrogging means offering something so much better that it overcomes all of the leader's network advantages. The iPhone leapfrogged older phones. ChatGPT leapfrogged traditional chatbots.

Watch out · Too big?

Winner-take-all markets create very powerful firms. That raises questions about fair competition and corporate responsibility, and regulators are paying close attention.

Section 7.5

Competing When Network Effects Matter

Strategy playbook
StrategyWhat it meansExample
Move earlyBuild the network before rivals doeBay in online auctions

Go viralLet users recruit users, using social proof“Join your friends on…” invites

Be compatibleUse backward compatibility or adaptors to tap an existing networkNew consoles that play older games
Use distributionShip your product where users already areMicrosoft putting AI into Windows and Bing
Watch out · The Osborne effect

Announcing a great new product too early can backfire. Customers stop buying today's version and wait. It's named after Osborne Computer, which reportedly sank after preannouncing its next model.

Watch out · Congestion effects

Sometimes more users make things worse, like clogged networks, spam, or crowded marketplaces. That's a congestion effect.

Sections 7.6–7.7 · Cases

Cases: Apple’s Platform Banks, and the Zoom Boom

Case · Apple cashes in on banking

Apple uses its huge iPhone platform to offer Apple Pay, Apple Cash, Apple Card, and savings accounts. Every iPhone owner is already in the network, so Apple can reach customers that banks spend heavily to win (high customer acquisition costs). Banks have pushed back with their own digital wallets, but competing with a platform already on billions of devices is hard.

Case · Zoom beats the big guys

Eric Yuan was an engineering leader at Cisco's WebEx. When Cisco wouldn't rebuild it, he left and founded Zoom. Zoom won with a simpler, more reliable product, a generous freemium version, easy pricing, and no account required to join. When COVID-19 hit, Zoom became a verb, beating giants like Cisco, Microsoft, and Google.

Takeaway

Video calling has weak network effects (anyone can join any meeting with a link). That's why a better product could win. Where network effects are strong, product quality alone usually isn't enough.

Vocabulary

Key Terms

Want every term from every module in one place? Try the searchable glossary.

Network effects
When a product or service becomes more valuable as more people use it. Also called Metcalfe's Law.
Platform
A product or service that others can build on, creating an ecosystem of add-ons. Examples: Windows, iOS, and game consoles.
Staying power
A product's long-term viability. Users stick with networks they believe will last.
Complementary benefits
Extra value from add-on products and services built for a platform.
Total cost of ownership (TCO)
The full cost of owning a product, including purchase price plus training, support, and maintenance.
Daily active users (DAU)
The average number of unique people who use a product each day.
Customer acquisition cost
How much a firm spends to win one new customer.
One-sided market
A network where value comes mostly from one type of user, like a messaging app.
Two-sided market
A network with two distinct groups that need each other, like players and game developers.
Same-side exchange benefit
Value users get from more users of the same type joining.
Cross-side exchange benefit
When growth on one side of a market attracts more participants on the other side.
Technological leapfrogging
Beating an established network with a product so superior that it outweighs the leader's advantages.
Social proof
The influence people feel when they see others doing something.
Envelopment
Taking over a market by making a rival's product a feature of your own.
Blue ocean strategy
Creating and competing in a new, uncontested market space instead of fighting crowded rivals.
Backward compatibility
When a new product can use the complements (apps, games, files) made for an older version.
Osborne effect
When announcing a future product causes sales of current products to drop as customers wait.
Congestion effect
When more users lower a product's value, for example through spam or crowding.
Freemium
Offering a free version of a product to attract users, then charging for premium features.
Metaverse
Immersive, shared digital worlds where people interact through avatars.
