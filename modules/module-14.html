The Big Picture

  • Every web request answers three questions: What are you looking for (the URL)? Where is it (the IP address, found through DNS)? How do we get there (TCP/IP and routers)?
  • Data travels in small packets that can take different routes and are reassembled at the end. That design makes the Internet fault-tolerant.
  • The slowest link is usually the last mile to your home, whether that's DSL, cable, fiber, 5G, or satellite.
  • Net neutrality asks whether ISPs should treat all traffic equally. It's still debated.
  • Low-orbit satellite networks like Starlink bring Internet access to remote places and concentrate a lot of power in one firm.

Section 14.2

The URL: “What Are You Looking For?”

A URL (web address) names a resource and the protocol used to get it. Here's one taken apart:

Protocol

http vs. https

HTTP lets browsers and servers talk. The “s” in HTTPS means the link is encrypted (originally with SSL, now its successor, TLS). Look for it before typing a password.

Domains

Who's in charge?

ICANN manages domain names. Top-level domains include .com, country codes like .uk (ccTLDs), and restricted ones like .edu (sTLDs).

Watch out

Cybersquatting

Registering a domain that uses someone else's trademark, hoping to profit. It's illegal in many countries.

Section 14.2

IP Addresses and DNS: “Where Is It?”

Computers find each other with numeric IP addresses, like 192.168.10.5 (four numbers from 0 to 255). The DNS (domain name system) is the Internet's phonebook: it translates waketech.edu into an IP address.

  1. You type a URLYour browser first checks its cache (temporary memory) to see if it already knows the IP address.
  2. Ask DNSIf not, a chain of DNS servers looks up the domain and returns its IP address.
  3. ConnectYour browser contacts the server at that IP address and requests the page.
The Internet is full! (sort of)

The original IPv4 system has only about 4.3 billion addresses, and we've run out. NAT lets many home devices share one public address. The newer IPv6 system has trillions upon trillions of addresses.

Section 14.3

TCP/IP: “How Do We Get There?”

Everything sent online, whether it's a page, an email, or a video, is chopped into small packets. Each one is stamped with where it's going.

The Internet's key protocols
ProtocolJobUsed for
IPAddresses packets and forwards them toward their destinationEverything
TCPMakes sure every packet arrives, in order, with no errors, and resends any that get lostWeb pages, email, file downloads
UDPSends fast with no delivery guaranteesLive video calls, gaming, streaming, where speed beats perfection

Routers connect networks and pass packets along. Different ISPs swap traffic through peering, often in shared buildings called colocation facilities. You can watch the path your packets take with a tool called traceroute.

Circuit vs. packet switching · VoIP

Old phone calls reserved a dedicated circuit for the whole call (circuit switching). The Internet shares lines among many users' packets (packet switching), which is much more efficient. VoIP makes phone calls just another Internet app. That's how WhatsApp and Zoom calls work.

Real-world example · Finance needs speed

High-frequency traders pay huge sums to put their servers right next to stock exchanges and use the fastest routes, because a few millionths of a second can mean millions of dollars. Automated trading can also cause sudden market crashes.

Section 14.4

The Last Mile: Faster Speed, Broader Access

The Internet backbone (including undersea cables) is incredibly fast. The bottleneck is usually the last mile into homes and businesses. Amdahl's Law says a system is only as fast as its slowest part. Bandwidth (speed) is measured in bits per second.

Last-mile broadband options
TechnologyHow it worksTrade-offs
DSLPhone company copper wiresWidely available but slow, and it slows down with distance
CableCoaxial cable from TV providersFast, but neighbors share capacity
Fiber (FTTH)Light through glass fiber to the homeFastest, but expensive to install
5G and mobileCell towers using licensed wireless spectrumMobile. Speeds vary a lot by type of 5G.
Wi-Fi and BluetoothShort-range wireless on unlicensed spectrumGreat locally, but not a last-mile solution by itself
SatelliteGEO (high orbit) or LEO (low orbit) satellitesReaches remote areas. GEO has high delay (latency).
Debate · Net neutrality

Net neutrality is the idea that ISPs should treat all traffic equally, without blocking, slowing, or charging extra for certain sites. Supporters say it protects free speech, competition, and startups. Critics say it discourages ISPs from investing in faster networks. U.S. rules have flipped back and forth with each administration. A federal court struck down the latest federal rules in early 2025, and some states have their own laws.

Section 14.5

To Infinity and Beyond: SpaceX Starlink

Three ways to deliver connectivity
TerrestrialGEO satellitesLEO satellites
WhereOn the ground (fiber, cable, cell towers)About 22,000 miles up, fixed over one spotUnder 1,200 miles up (Starlink is about 340 miles)
DelayLowestHighLow
Satellites neededNoneA few cover much of the globeThousands, constantly moving

SpaceX's reusable rockets made it affordable to launch thousands of Starlink satellites. The service now connects rural homes, ships, planes, disaster zones, and even battlefields.

Watch out · Power and space junk

When one company, and one person, controls critical infrastructure used by militaries and governments, it raises serious questions about accountability. Thousands of satellites also raise the risk of the Kessler effect: a chain reaction of space-debris collisions.

Vocabulary

Key Terms

Want every term from every module in one place? Try the searchable glossary.

ISP
Internet service provider: a company that provides Internet access.
URL
A web address that identifies a resource and the protocol used to retrieve it.
Protocol
Agreed-upon rules and formats that let devices communicate.
HTTP / HTTPS
The protocol browsers and web servers use to talk. HTTPS adds encryption.
SSL
Secure Sockets Layer: a security standard that encrypts the link between a browser and a server. Today's version is called TLS.
ICANN
The nonprofit that manages the Internet's domain name and numbering systems.
Cybersquatting
Registering a domain using someone else's trademark or name to profit from it.
HTML
Hypertext Markup Language: the language used to build web pages.
IP address
A numeric address that identifies a device on the Internet.
DNS
Domain name system: the Internet's directory, which translates domain names into IP addresses.
NAT
Network address translation: lets many devices on a private network share one public IP address.
Cache
Temporary storage that speeds up repeated tasks.
Load balancing
Spreading work across multiple servers to avoid slowdowns.
Fault tolerance
A system's ability to keep working even when a part fails.
Packet
A small unit of data sent across a network.
TCP
Transmission Control Protocol: makes sure all packets arrive correctly and in order.
IP (Internet Protocol)
The protocol that addresses and routes packets across the Internet.
UDP
User Datagram Protocol: a faster protocol without delivery guarantees, used for live video and gaming.
Router
A device that connects networks and forwards packets between them.
VoIP
Voice over IP: phone calls carried over the Internet.
Peering
When ISPs connect their networks directly to swap traffic.
Last mile
The connection between the Internet and a home or business. It's usually the slowest part.
Bandwidth
How much data a connection can carry, measured in bits per second.
Broadband
A general term for high-speed Internet connections.
Fiber to the home (FTTH)
Broadband delivered over fiber-optic cables to the home.
Wireless spectrum
The radio frequencies used for wireless communication.
Net neutrality
The principle that ISPs should treat all Internet traffic equally.
LEO
Low Earth orbit: satellites under about 1,200 miles up, offering low delay.
GEO
Geosynchronous Earth orbit: satellites about 22,000 miles up that stay over one spot.
Kessler effect
A feared chain reaction of space-debris collisions creating more debris.
