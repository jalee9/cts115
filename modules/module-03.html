The Big Picture

  • Moore's Law: chip performance per dollar doubles about every 18 months. Computing keeps getting faster and cheaper.
  • As tech gets cheaper, demand explodes (price elasticity). That opens new markets and can disrupt whole industries.
  • Chips are hitting physical limits, so engineers keep computing fast and cheap other ways: multicore chips, parallel computing, and the cloud.
  • Fast, cheap tech puts smarts into everyday objects. That's the Internet of Things.
  • The dark side: rapid upgrades create mountains of e-waste, and firms are responsible for their products' whole lifecycle.
Your goals

Section 3.1

Moore’s Law: Faster, Cheaper, Every Year

In 1965, Intel co-founder Gordon Moore noticed that the number of transistors on a chip kept doubling. Today, managers use Moore's Law to mean that chip performance per dollar doubles about every 18 months. Other tech moves fast too, including storage (hard drives and flash memory) and fiber-optic network speeds.

Key idea

Price elasticity

When prices fall, demand for price-elastic products like electronics shoots up. Cheaper chips mean smartphones for billions, not just thousands.

Key idea

Plan for change

Expensive tech today will be cheap tomorrow. Managers must plan inventory, pricing, and strategy knowing their gear will lose value quickly.

Key idea

Disruption ahead

Products that once needed special hardware (cameras, GPS, music players) collapsed into one cheap phone.

Real-world example · Mobile money in Somaliland

Cheap mobile phones turned Somaliland into a largely cashless economy. Farmers, small shops, and large businesses buy and sell with phone-based payments, which makes daily life easier and helps connect the economy to the rest of the world.

Real-world example · A smart pill bottle

The Internet of Things reaches the medicine cabinet. AdhereTech's smart bottle has sensors that record when it's opened and sends reminders if a patient misses a dose. The patient doesn't have to learn anything new to use it.

Section 3.1

Speaking Tech: Memory, Storage, and Data Sizes

A few terms come up again and again. Knowing them helps you talk confidently with tech teams.

Memory vs. storage
TermWhat it isLoses data when power is off?
RAMFast chip-based memory the computer uses while it worksYes (volatile)
Flash memory / SSDChip-based storage in phones, laptops, and USB drives. It has no moving parts (solid state).No (nonvolatile)
Hard diskSpinning magnetic storage. It's cheap per gigabyte but slower and more fragile.No (nonvolatile)

How big is that? Data size units

  1. BitA single 1 or 0, the smallest piece of digital data.
  2. Byte = 8 bitsAbout one typed character.
  3. Kilobyte (KB) ≈ a thousand bytesA short text email.
  4. Megabyte (MB) ≈ a million bytesA phone photo or a song.
  5. Gigabyte (GB) ≈ a billion bytesA movie.
  6. Terabyte (TB) ≈ a trillion bytesA large laptop drive.
  7. Petabyte (PB), Exabyte (EB), Zettabyte (ZB)Each is about 1,000 times bigger than the last. This is “big data” and global Internet scale.
Watch out · Bits vs. bytes

Internet speeds are measured in bits per second (Mbps), but file sizes are in bytes (MB). A lowercase “b” means bits; an uppercase “B” means bytes. Mixing them up makes downloads look eight times faster than they are.

Section 3.2

The End of Moore’s Law? Not the End of Fast, Cheap Computing

Chips are made of semiconductors (mostly silicon) in multibillion-dollar factories called fabs, where hundreds of chips are etched onto each silicon wafer. As transistors shrink toward the size of atoms, two problems grow: heat and physical size limits.

Workaround

Multicore chips

Put two or more lower-power processor “cores” on one chip. But software must be written to use all of the cores at once.

Workaround

Specialized chips

Chips built for one job, like Nvidia's GPUs for AI, can beat general-purpose chips by a wide margin.

Future

Quantum computing

Quantum computers use “qubits” instead of plain 1s and 0s. They're promising for chemistry and medicine, but still experimental and years away.

Why it matters

Progress in AI is moving even faster than Moore's Law, driven by specialized chips and huge data centers. Firms that can access that computing power gain a big edge.

Section 3.3

The Power of Parallel: Supercomputers, Grids, Clusters & Clouds

Instead of making one chip faster, connect many processors and split the work. This is called massively parallel computing.

Four ways to combine computing power
ApproachHow it works
SupercomputerOne of the world's fastest computers when it's built, with thousands of processors working together.
Grid computingSoftware links many (often idle) computers so they act like one giant machine.
Cluster computingServers connected by software and networking to share work, often in racks of thin “blade” servers.
Cloud computingRenting computing power or software over the Internet from huge server farms, like Software as a Service (SaaS).
Watch out · Not every job splits nicely

Parallel computing works best when a problem can be divided into independent pieces. Tasks where each step depends on the last, or where moving data across a network adds delay (latency), don't speed up as much.

Section 3.4

E-Waste: The Dark Side of Moore’s Law

Fast, cheap tech means we replace devices quickly. Discarded electronics (e-waste) contain toxic materials like lead and mercury. Much of it ends up in landfills or is shipped to poorer countries, where workers may strip it without protection.

Problem

Hard to recycle

Devices are built small, light, and glued together. That's great for buyers and terrible for recyclers.

Progress

Recycling robots

Apple built a robot named Daisy that takes apart its own iPhones to recover valuable materials.

Responsibility

Know your partners

A firm's reputation can suffer if its e-waste is found dumped overseas. Managers should vet recyclers and ask hard ethical questions.

Section 3.5 · Case

Case: Disney’s MagicBand

Disney World turned a simple wristband into a powerful information system. The MagicBand uses wireless chips to serve as a guest's park ticket, hotel room key, ride reservation, and payment method. Newer versions add lights and haptic (vibration) feedback.

For guests

Less friction

Fewer lines, no wallet, and personalized surprises like characters greeting kids by name.

For Disney

Big data

Disney sees how guests move through the park, so it can staff better, shorten waits, and encourage spending.

The catch

Big cost, big coordination

The system reportedly cost around a billion dollars and required many departments to work together.

Takeaway

Cheap chips let firms embed technology into experiences that used to be manual. The payoff can be huge, but large systems are expensive, complex, and only work if the whole organization gets behind them.

Vocabulary

Key Terms

Want every term from every module in one place? Try the searchable glossary.

Moore's Law
The observation that chip performance per dollar doubles about every 18 months.
Semiconductor
A material, usually silicon, that can allow or block electricity. It's what computer chips are made of.
Price elasticity
How much demand changes when price changes. Demand for price-elastic goods like electronics jumps when prices fall.
RAM
Fast, chip-based working memory. It's volatile, so it's wiped when power is cut.
Volatile memory
Storage that loses its data when the power goes off, like RAM.
Nonvolatile memory
Storage that keeps data without power, like flash memory or a hard disk.
Flash memory
Chip-based, nonvolatile storage used in phones, cameras, and USB drives.
SSD
Solid state drive: chip-based nonvolatile storage with no moving parts.
Solid state
Electronics with no moving parts. They usually fail less and use less energy.
Latency
Delay. Low-latency systems respond faster.
Internet of Things (IoT)
Low-cost sensors, chips, and network connections built into everyday products so they can collect data and act automatically.
Fab
A semiconductor fabrication plant: a multibillion-dollar factory that makes chips.
Multicore processor
A chip with two or more processor cores working side by side.
Grid computing
Software that links many, often idle, computers so they work together like one supercomputer.
Cluster computing
Servers connected by software and networking to tackle computing tasks together.
Cloud computing
Using hardware or software delivered as a service over the Internet instead of owning it.
Software as a Service (SaaS)
Software a firm subscribes to and uses online instead of installing it.
E-waste
Discarded electronic devices, which often contain toxic materials.
Haptic
Related to touch, like the vibration your phone or watch gives as feedback.
