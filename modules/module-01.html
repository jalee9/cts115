The Big Picture

  • Technology isn't just an IT topic. It's changing every industry, every job, and every career, including yours.
  • Fast tech change brings promise and peril: new opportunities, and also job disruption, misinformation, and privacy risks.
  • Tech giants have created enormous wealth, so regulators around the world are now working to limit their power.
  • Many of the biggest tech firms were started by people in their twenties, and starting a business is cheaper than ever.
  • The Hype Cycle helps managers avoid jumping on a new technology too early or giving up on it too soon.
Your goals

Section 1.1

Tech’s Tectonic Shift: Business Is Changing Fast

A tectonic shift is a change so big it moves the ground under everyone’s feet. That’s what technology is doing to business. Some of the world’s most valuable companies are tech firms, and even “non-tech” businesses like banks, retailers, and hospitals now compete on how well they use technology.

Key idea

Tech firms are giants

Investors measure a company’s value with its market capitalization: the number of shares times the share price. Many of the largest companies by market cap are tech firms.

Key idea

AI is the hottest topic

AI runs on powerful microprocessors (chips) housed in huge server farms. That’s why chipmakers like Nvidia have become so important.

Key idea

Promise and peril

The same tech that cures diseases and cuts costs can also eliminate jobs, spread misinformation, and create new security and privacy threats.

Key idea

Regulators are pushing back

Governments are writing new rules to limit Big Tech’s power, and those rules can erase advantages that firms used to rely on.

Real-world example · Drones that save lives

The company Zipline uses small, self-flying drones to deliver blood to hospitals in Rwanda, reaching places that are slow to get to by road. Drones like these are electric and autonomous, so each light delivery can be faster and use less energy than sending a car.

Real-world example · Europe’s “gatekeeper” rules

The European Union named six companies gatekeepers: Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Meta, and Microsoft. These firms now face extra rules. For example, they can’t favor their own services over rivals on their platforms, and they may have to make their messaging apps work with competitors’ apps. Their products in Europe may end up working differently than everywhere else.

Why it matters to you

Your career will be full of constant change, much of it caused by technology. Learning new tech and judging its impact isn’t a one-time class. It’s a lifelong habit.

Section 1.2

It’s Your Revolution: Young Founders & Startups

Many of the most influential tech firms were started by people in their twenties or younger, often while still in school. Facebook, Google, Dell, and Microsoft all began this way. Cloud computing, open-source software, and online tools have made it cheaper than ever to launch a business.

A typical startup journey

  1. Spot a problemFind a need people will pay to solve. Often it’s one you have yourself.
  2. Build & testLaunch a simple version quickly and learn from real users.
  3. Pivot if neededIf it isn’t working, pivot: significantly change the product, market, or business model.
  4. Get supportJoin a startup accelerator like Y Combinator for mentoring, connections, and early funding.
  5. Raise moneyInvestors and founders sign a term sheet that spells out how much is invested and who owns what.
Real-world example · Y Combinator alumni

Y Combinator is one of the best-known startup accelerators. Firms that went through it include Airbnb, Dropbox, Stripe, and Reddit. In return for funding and coaching, startups give the accelerator a small ownership stake.

Real-world example · Canva

Canva set out to make graphic design simple for non-designers. It now builds AI throughout its tools, so users can edit photos and create designs with generative AI, which pushes that “easy design” mission even further.

Section 1.3

Geek Up: Tech Is in Every Career

No matter your major, technology will shape your job. Every business discipline now depends on data, software, and increasingly AI. Tech-focused careers are also varied, well paid, and growing.

How technology shows up across business disciplines
Discipline How tech is changing it
FinanceAI spots fraud, runs trading, and personalizes banking. Big banks treat AI as a core strategy.
AccountingAutomation and AI handle routine work like auditing and data entry, so accountants focus on analysis and advice.
MarketingData drives targeting and measurement. Brands even use computer-generated “virtual influencers.”
OperationsSensors and AI predict machine breakdowns, check quality automatically, and flag problems in real time.
Human ResourcesMany employers use AI to screen job applicants. New laws require them to check those tools for bias.
LawAI legal assistants review documents, do research, and summarize long contracts.
Information SystemsAI now helps programmers write code, and tech careers keep growing.
Watch out · AI can be biased

An AI hiring tool learns from past data. If that data reflects past bias (for example, by race or gender), the AI can repeat it at scale. That’s why places like New York City now require employers to audit AI hiring tools and be open about using them.

Fun concept · The “uncanny valley”

As computer-generated faces look more human, people like them more, until they look almost real. Then people suddenly feel uneasy. That “cute to creepy” dip is called the uncanny valley, and marketers using virtual influencers have to work around it.

Section 1.4

See Beyond the Hype: The Hype Cycle

New technologies usually arrive with a burst of excitement. Then reality hits. The Hype Cycle, popularized by the research firm Gartner, is a picture of that pattern. Managers use it to ask, “Is this tech overhyped right now, or is it ready to deliver real value?”

  1. Innovation TriggerA breakthrough, demo, or launch gets people talking. There are few real products yet.
  2. Peak of Inflated ExpectationsMedia buzz and investment explode. Expectations get way ahead of what the tech can actually do.
  3. Trough of DisillusionmentEarly projects disappoint, interest crashes, and weak firms fail.
  4. Slope of EnlightenmentPeople figure out where the tech truly works. Better versions appear.
  5. Plateau of ProductivityThe tech goes mainstream and delivers dependable value.
Why it matters to a manager

The Hype Cycle helps you avoid four costly mistakes: buying in too early, buying in too late, giving up too soon, or hanging on too long.

Watch out · It’s a guess, not a measurement

The Hype Cycle isn’t based on hard data or math. It’s an educated judgment. Placing a technology on the curve takes research and reasoning, and the frameworks in the rest of this course will help you do that.

Vocabulary

Key Terms

Want every term from every module in one place? Try the searchable glossary.

Market capitalization (market cap)
A public company’s total value, found by multiplying its number of shares by the current share price.
R&D
Research and development: the part of a company focused on inventing new products and technologies.
Microprocessor
The chip that carries out a computer program’s instructions. It’s the computer’s “brain.”
Server farm
A huge collection of networked servers working together. Server farms power cloud services and large websites.
Pivot
When a startup significantly changes its product, market, or business model because the original plan isn’t working.
Term sheet
A document between a startup and its investors that lays out the investment terms, such as how much ownership the investors get.
Startup accelerator
A program (like Y Combinator) that gives young startups mentoring, connections, and seed money, usually in exchange for a small ownership stake.
Gatekeeper (EU)
The EU’s label for a few giant tech platforms that must follow extra competition rules, such as not favoring their own services.
Hype Cycle
A curve that shows how excitement about a new technology rises, crashes, and then recovers as real uses emerge.
Peak of Inflated Expectations
The Hype Cycle stage where buzz and investment are highest and expectations outrun what the tech can actually do.
Trough of Disillusionment
The Hype Cycle stage where early results disappoint, interest drops, and weaker firms fail.
Uncanny valley
The uneasy feeling people get when a computer-generated face looks almost, but not quite, human.
